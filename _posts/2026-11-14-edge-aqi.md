This post is about [EdgeAQI](https://github.com/NishTheFish92/Edge_AQI), a
course project my teammates and I built for our IoT subjects, how it's put
together and the parts that took the most work.

## The idea

We wanted to record air quality across an area and see which spots were more
polluted than others. The end goal was a heatmap of the area we walked through.

Instead of sending everything straight from the sensor to a server, we went
with a three tier architecture: an edge device, a fog node and a cloud. A fog
node is an intermediate node that processes data from the edge device before
it goes to the cloud.

## Edge: the ESP32

The edge device is an ESP32 with an MQ-135 gas sensor and a DHT11 for
temperature and humidity.

It samples based on how polluted the air is and how fast that's changing. In
clean air it takes a reading every 2 seconds, which goes down to 1 second and
then 0.5 seconds as CO2 rises. If CO2 jumps by more than 100 ppm between two
readings, it switches to fast sampling straight away. Every 5 samples it
averages them and sends that one reading to the phone.

## Fog: a phone running Python

We needed a reliable way to run a simple Python script on a phone, so we used
[Termux](https://termux.dev/), which gives you a Linux terminal on Android. With
the Termux:API package, `termux-location` gives you GPS coordinates straight
from the terminal. Another way to do this would have been a small Android app,
but a Python script was much quicker to get running.

The phone does two things with each reading:

- **Cleans outliers.** It keeps the last 20 readings and drops any new one that
  is more than 4 standard deviations from their mean. It only starts filtering
  once it has 10 readings to compare against. Rejected readings still go into
  the window, so if the air really does change and stays that way, the filter
  catches up after a few readings instead of rejecting everything from then on.
- **Adds a GPS tag** and forwards the reading. Since the phone has GPS, the
  ESP32 doesn't need its own GPS module.

## Cloud: a laptop behind ngrok

The "cloud" was a laptop running a Flask server, exposed to the phone through
an [ngrok](https://ngrok.com/) link. It averages every 10 readings into one row
in a SQLite database and redraws the heatmap with Folium after each new row.

The coordinates are averaged too, so each point on the map is the average
location of those 10 readings, not one exact spot. Between the ESP32 and the
laptop, 50 raw samples go into each point. That also means points are closer
together where the air is worse. In clean air one point covers about 100
seconds of walking, and in polluted air about 25.

## Hard part 1: calibrating the MQ-135

Most of the effort went into getting the MQ-135 to give a sensible value. It
doesn't actually measure CO2. It reacts to a mix of gases, and a curve from its
datasheet turns that into an estimated CO2 value.

That curve needs a baseline called R0, the sensor's resistance in clean air.
Finding it was mostly a lot of playing around with formulas. Our calibration
script averages the sensor's resistance over 150 readings in clean air and
writes R0 to the ESP32's EEPROM, so it survives restarts and the main firmware
just reads it on boot.

Even calibrated, the numbers aren't accurate CO2 levels. What they are good for
is comparing places with each other, which is all a heatmap needs. The map
scales its colours between the lowest and highest values in the data, so it
shows where the air is relatively worse, not whether it's safe.

## Hard part 2: actually collecting the data

The other hard part was running around campus with the ESP32 and the phone,
hoping everything was being recorded properly the whole time. Thankfully it
all worked out so not too much running around :)

## Looking back

The three tiers made the project more interesting than a sensor posting
straight to a server. Each layer has one job, and the phone turned out to be a
very capable fog node.

If I did it again, I'd use a proper gas sensor for CO2 and replace the laptop
and ngrok with a real server. I'd also swap the Termux script for a proper
Android app. That would make it much easier to use native Android APIs, like
Bluetooth instead of WiFi between the ESP32 and the phone.
